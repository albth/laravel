Astrocyte–vessel interfaces · MICrONS minnie65 + literature + your TRAP-seq

A cell body on a vessel is not a big endfoot

Endfeet are thin, remote, anucleate outposts that run on local translation and oxidative metabolism. A soma contact puts the nucleus, the bulk secretory machinery and the glycogen store directly against the basal lamina. Same cell, same number of endfeet — a different kind of interface.

Geometry measured in 240 reconstructed astrocytes · molecular evidence from published compartment proteomics · hypotheses flagged separately from findings

Why the two interfaces cannot do the same job

The asymmetry is not about size. It is about what each compartment contains, and published compartment proteomics make the split unusually concrete: when astrocyte endfeet were purified and compared with astrocyte somata, electron transport chain proteins were found at the endfeet while glycogen-handling enzymes stayed in the cell body.

Endfoot

A remote, self-supplying outpost

  • Oxidative. Electron transport chain components were detected at endfeet; the authors read this as subcellular metabolic compartmentalisation. published
  • Translationally autonomous. Endfeet carry their own mRNA and synthesise protein locally, including Aqp4, Kcnj10, Slc1a2 and Gja1, with local smooth and rough ER and Golgi. published
  • Anucleate and thin. Median 88 µm² in typical cells — a patch roughly 6 µm long on a capillary. measured
  • Scaffold-dependent. AQP4 is held in the membrane by the dystrophin-associated complex (dystroglycan, α-syntrophin, dystrobrevin, dystrophin). Lose the scaffold and the interface fails in place. published
  • Far from the nucleus. A blood-borne signal arriving here is ~23 µm from the genome. measured
Soma contact

The whole cell, pressed on the vessel

  • Glycolytic / storage. Glycogen-handling enzymes are distributed mostly to the soma — so a soma contact puts the cell's fuel store against the glucose source. published
  • Nucleus at the basal lamina. Under 1 µm from the lumen, versus a 9.4 µm median for astrocytes generally. measured
  • Bulk secretory apparatus. The main ER, Golgi and centrosome sit here, not in the endfoot. published
  • Large and continuous. 525 µm² median, about 10× a single endfoot, and 3× more vessel-facing membrane per cell overall. measured
  • Cell-cycle competent. After injury it is specifically the astrocytes whose somata touch vessels that proliferate; no astrocyte was seen migrating to the lesion. published

How each interface fails

Mapping disease onto compartments separates two very different failure modes. Endfoot disease is a transport and barrier problem at a structure that is already in place. Soma-contact disease is a cell-fate problem: the cell at the vessel changes what it is.

Endfoot failure — losing a surface
  • Stroke oedema. Cation flux through SUR1–TRPM4 and NCX1 in endfeet drives water influx through AQP4 and brain swelling after ischaemia. published
  • Alzheimer's and CAA. AQP4 leaves endfoot membranes around amyloid-laden vessels and rises in the surrounding neuropil; perivascular AQP4 loss tracks AD status, plaque and tangle burden, and reduced glymphatic clearance. published
  • But not simply harmful. In a prion–CAA model, displacing AQP4 from endfeet prolonged survival and slowed synaptic loss — depolarisation is not a one-way road to damage. published
  • Channelopathy. The endfoot is where AQP4 and Kir4.1 are concentrated, making it the structure at risk in AQP4-antibody disease and in KCNJ10 loss-of-function.
Soma-contact failure — changing a cell
  • Proliferation after injury. Juxtavascular astrocytes are the ones that re-enter the cell cycle after a stab wound; the increase in astrocyte number is local proliferation, not migration. published
  • Barrier damage as the trigger. In pathologies that disrupt vessels, proliferation stays restricted to the juxtavascular niche — implicating BBB dysfunction itself as the cue. published
  • A homeostatic cost. Cell-cycle re-entry comes with Kir4.1 downregulation, especially in juxtavascular astrocytes — the cells best placed to buffer potassium stop doing it when they divide. published
  • Gatekeeping, proposed. That juxtavascular proliferation walls off immune-cell invasion at the vascular interface is an attractive idea but, on the evidence available here, still a hypothesis. hypothesis

The asymmetry worth noticing

The endfoot has a molecular inventory, a scaffold, named channels and a decade of disease mechanism. The soma contact has essentially one established finding — it proliferates after injury — and almost no molecular characterisation. That gap is where your question lives, and it is why the geometry above matters: it says the soma contact is a large, common, specifically-positioned structure that nobody has profiled.

Six hypotheses, and how to kill them

Each follows from a specific measured or published asymmetry rather than from the general idea that contact matters.

1
Latency: the soma contact is a fast line from blood to genome

A signal arriving at an endfoot is ~23 µm from the nucleus and must be relayed; arriving at a soma contact it is under 1 µm. If transcriptional responses to blood-borne cues are diffusion- or relay-limited, vascular-coupled astrocytes should respond sooner and more strongly than their neighbours.

Test Acute systemic challenge (LPS, hypoxia, or a circulating cytokine), then nascent-transcript smFISH or intron-FISH for immediate-early and NF-κB target genes, scored per cell against measured nucleus–lumen distance. Prediction: induction amplitude falls off with distance, with the steepest difference in the first 15–30 min.
2
Niche: the vessel is where an astrocyte must sit to divide

Mitosis needs the nucleus and somewhere to put two daughters. Only soma-contacting astrocytes have both at the vessel, which would explain why proliferation after injury is juxtavascular without requiring a special signalling state — position, not identity.

Test In a vessel-disrupting lesion, ask whether the pre-injury nucleus–lumen distance of each astrocyte predicts its probability of dividing, using live imaging or a cell-cycle reporter. The positional model predicts a sharp threshold near contact; an identity model predicts a marker that is already different before injury.
3
Secretion: vectorial release into the perivascular space

Endfeet translate locally but the bulk ER–Golgi route is at the soma. An astrocyte whose soma is on the basal lamina can deliver newly made secreted and membrane protein straight into the perivascular compartment, rather than trafficking it 23 µm down a process.

Test Retention-using-selective-hooks (RUSH) release of a secreted astrocyte cargo, imaged at the vessel; or Golgi/centrosome position relative to the contact by correlative EM. Prediction: in vc-astrocytes the Golgi is polarised toward the vessel face.
4
Fuel logistics: the glycogen store sits on the glucose source

Glycogen enzymes are a soma compartment and the electron transport chain is an endfoot compartment. Putting the soma on the vessel co-locates storage with supply, which should change how fast the cell can mobilise glycogen during activity or hypoglycaemia.

Test Glycogen histochemistry or a genetically encoded glucose/lactate sensor during an insulin-hypoglycaemia or high-activity challenge, comparing vc and non-vc astrocytes in the same field.
5
Real estate: the soma competes with microglia for the vessel surface

Microglial somata show the strongest vessel-contact enrichment of any cell type measured here (6.8× chance, versus 2.5× for astrocytes), and microglia are known to reach the vasculature through gaps between astrocyte endfeet. A 525 µm² astrocyte sheath is a large occupancy of exactly that surface.

Test In the same EM volume, ask whether astrocyte soma contacts and microglial contacts are spatially exclusive along individual capillaries, against a null that randomises positions along the same vessel. This is directly answerable in the dataset already reconstructed.
6
Mixture: a specialised population diluted by chance contacts

Soma contact is only 2.5× more common than chance placement predicts, so roughly 40% of contacting astrocytes are expected to be touching a vessel incidentally. Any functional measure should therefore be bimodal, and group means will understate the real effect.

Test Treat contact as a graded variable — area and circumferential coverage of the sheath, not a yes/no label — and look for a discontinuity. Large, wrapping sheaths should separate from small incidental appositions on any marker that works.

What your TRAP-seq can and cannot say yet

The JD130 (pan-astrocyte) versus JD1884 (vcAst) comparison was the obvious place to look for the molecular signature of a soma contact. It cannot currently be used for that, and the reason is visible in one plot.

What survives a proper correction

Because the contaminating signal inflates broadly-expressed genes and deflates astrocyte-specific ones, any gene's apparent fold change mostly reports how astrocyte-restricted it is. Restricting to astrocyte-specific genes and renormalising on astrocyte content (median-of-ratios size factors computed only on those genes) removes the artefact — and leaves very little.
